# Experiments

The experiments were structured around a small number of meaningful design changes rather than around architecture names alone. One of the earliest substantial architectural changes was the introduction of a bidirectional GRU on top of the convolutional frontend. The motivation was straightforward: local tablature decisions often depend on short-range temporal structure, and a bidirectional recurrent layer can condition each representation on both preceding and following frames within the context window. In principle, this should help the model resolve sustained notes, transitions, and ambiguous local spectral patterns more effectively than a purely feedforward convolutional stack.

In practice, however, the bidirectional design appeared to be too large for the scale and regularity of the dataset. It substantially increased recurrent capacity and seemed to fit the training behavior more aggressively than the downstream evaluation metrics justified. Even when the model produced strong pitch-level results, the gain on the stricter tablature metrics was not decisive enough to clearly warrant the extra complexity. This influenced the next steps in two ways. First, it motivated a search for a simpler recurrent alternative that would preserve temporal modeling while reducing the risk of overfitting. Second, it sharpened attention on whether the observed limitations were architectural or optimization-related.

The next major issue that emerged was a dominant-class failure mode. After changes to the training stack, both the CNN and CRNN could become overly conservative and collapse toward the non-sounding class. The empirical signature of this failure was clear: precision remained superficially high, but recall dropped sharply, and qualitative predictions often contained too few active strings. This result influenced the rest of the project in an essential way, because it showed that no architectural comparison would be trustworthy until the optimization behavior was understood and corrected. The underlying intuition was that a per-string classifier trained on heavily imbalanced labels will often discover that predicting silence is a low-risk strategy.

The response to this failure was therefore not primarily architectural but objective-driven. Later iterations emphasized recovering sounding notes more reliably rather than simply accepting the conservative operating point. Once that behavior was recognized and partially mitigated, the interpretation of the experiments changed. The relevant question was no longer whether the model could predict notes at all, but how different temporal modeling choices traded off recall, precision, and exact fingering accuracy.

The next major change was to replace the bidirectional GRU with a smaller unidirectional GRU. This was motivated by two observations. First, the bidirectional model appeared to carry more complexity than the dataset could justify cleanly, making overfitting a plausible concern. Second, for the final comparison, the goal was not to maximize recurrent capacity but to isolate the effect of adding modest temporal memory to an otherwise comparable convolutional model. The unidirectional GRU preserved the main theoretical advantage of recurrence, namely the ability to propagate information forward through neighboring frames, while reducing model size and making the comparison against the CNN more interpretable. Empirically, this simplified CRNN improved pitch recall further and achieved the best pitch F1 among the final runs, around 0.84. At the same time, it still did not surpass the CNN on the most exact tablature-oriented metrics.

Another meaningful change in the later recurrent experiments was the introduction of sounding-aware supervision. The motivation was that the full 21-class per-string prediction problem conflates two related but distinct questions: whether a string is active at all, and if active, which fret is sounding. By encouraging the model to represent sounding versus non-sounding states more explicitly, the auxiliary loss was intended to counteract the dominant mute-class bias and improve note recovery. The observed outcome was consistent with that intuition: later CRNN variants became stronger on recall-oriented and pitch-level metrics, suggesting that the model was less hesitant to predict sounding notes. However, this also reinforced a recurring pattern in the results: improvements in note presence detection did not automatically translate into better exact string-fret assignment.

These experimental changes collectively shaped the final conclusion. Addressing class collapse was necessary before any comparison could be taken seriously. Increasing recurrent capacity through a bidirectional GRU demonstrated that temporal modeling could help, but it did not yield a clean enough gain to justify the added complexity. Simplifying to a unidirectional GRU produced a more interpretable model and the strongest pitch-level performance, yet the CNN remained slightly better on exact tablature F1 and more clearly better on disambiguation rate. The experiments therefore suggest that temporal modeling mainly helps with continuity and sounding-note recovery, while the harder remaining problem is precise fingering disambiguation.
