# Conclusion And Future Work

This project reproduced a workable guitar tablature estimation pipeline on GuitarSet and used it to compare a convolution-only baseline against evolving convolutional recurrent extensions. The work progressed through several stages: modernization of the original code, diagnosis of an early class-collapse failure mode, initial experiments with a larger bidirectional-GRU CRNN, and a final stabilized comparison using a smaller unidirectional GRU. The main empirical result is that recurrence improves pitch-level transcription quality, especially recall and pitch F1, but does not consistently improve the stricter tablature metrics that depend on assigning notes to the correct string and fret. In the current comparison, the CRNN is better at recovering sounding notes, while the CNN remains slightly better on exact tablature F1 and more clearly better on tablature disambiguation rate.

One response to this failure mode is in changes to the objective function. Later iterations emphasized recovering sounding notes more reliably rather than simply accepting the conservative operating point. Once that behavior was recognized and partially mitigated, the interpretation of the experiments changed. The relevant question was no longer whether the model could predict notes at all, but how different temporal modeling choices traded off recall, precision, and exact fingering accuracy. The final objective-function adjustment within the simplified CRNN is consistent with that interpretation: reducing the closed-class weight from 0.5 to 0.25 increased pitch recall from 83.31\% to 84.99\% and tablature recall from 75.25\% to 76.22\%, while slightly improving pitch F1 from 84.06\% to 84.16\%. However, those gains came with lower pitch precision, lower tablature precision and F1, a lower tablature disambiguation rate, and a worse incorrect-note distance. In other words, the loss change pushed the model further toward aggressive note recovery, but it did not remove the underlying tradeoff between recovering more sounding events and assigning them to the correct string-fret position.

That outcome suggests that temporal modeling is useful but insufficient on its own. The remaining challenge is not just temporal continuity but instrument-specific ambiguity. A strong future direction is to tune losses more directly for tablature quality rather than relying mainly on pitch recovery and framewise classification. Additional experiments with class weighting, auxiliary sounding losses, and alternative spectral inputs such as mel or combined CQT-plus-mel features would also help clarify which parts of the pipeline matter most.

Longer-term work could move beyond framewise prediction entirely. Sequence decoding, note-level objectives, or stronger temporal architectures may improve consistency across time while respecting the combinatorial structure of guitar fingering. A future revisit to bidirectional or transformer-style sequence models would be more convincing once the simplified recurrent baseline and loss design are fully tuned. The current results make a narrower but useful claim: careful reproduction matters, recurrence is beneficial for pitch recovery, and exact tablature prediction remains the harder problem.
