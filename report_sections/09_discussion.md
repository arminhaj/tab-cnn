# Discussion

The results suggest that temporal recurrence helps the model recover sounding notes more consistently but does not solve the harder ambiguity of exact fingering assignment. This difference is visible in the gap between pitch-level gains and tablature-level gains. A recurrent layer can exploit the fact that neighboring frames often belong to the same note event or short musical gesture, which naturally improves continuity and recall. However, once the correct pitch has been identified, the string and fret choice may still be ambiguous from audio alone, especially when multiple valid fingerings produce the same pitch.

The project also highlights the importance of optimization details. Earlier failing runs indicate that this problem is sensitive to data-pipeline and runtime changes, particularly in the presence of a dominant non-sounding class. That sensitivity means architectural conclusions should be drawn carefully: a weak run does not always imply a weak model, and part of the empirical work here involved separating training regressions from genuine modeling limits.

The intermediate bidirectional-GRU phase is useful in this respect. It showed that a much larger recurrent model could produce strong results once collapse was mitigated, but it also raised the question of whether that extra capacity was justified. Because the later, smaller unidirectional GRU reached comparable or better pitch-level performance while remaining simpler, the project ultimately points toward a more controlled recurrent design rather than toward maximum temporal capacity.

Another important limitation is data scale. GuitarSet is valuable and well annotated, but it is still small relative to the datasets typically used to train modern sequence models. That makes hyperparameter choices, split design, and regularization decisions more influential. It also limits how confidently one can claim that a modest advantage in one metric will generalize beyond the benchmark.

Overall, the project shows that better local note tracking and better fingering prediction are related but distinct goals. It also shows that the path to a credible comparison required multiple experimental stages: reproduction, debugging collapse, trying a larger bidirectional recurrent model, and then simplifying the architecture to isolate the actual modeling tradeoff. If the application prioritizes pitch transcription, the CRNN is an attractive direction. If the application prioritizes exact tablature fidelity, the CNN remains competitive and may be easier to train and interpret.
